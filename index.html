Why it matters

Housing, transit, health care, schools and jobs all look different in a region with hundreds of people per square kilometre than in one with fewer than one. Conventional maps emphasize land area, which can obscure how concentrated the population is. Population cartograms highlight where people are concentrate.

About the data

  • Population: Statistics Canada, 2021 Census of Population (5,000,879 people). Census divisions are B.C.’s regional districts, plus Northern Rockies and Stikine.
  • Outline and land area: province outline from the Statistics Canada boundary file; division land areas are 2021 Census land-area figures (water excluded), which sum to the provincial land area of about 920,700 km².
  • Blocks: both maps use 1,000 equal blocks. Land allocations use each region’s share of 920,685 km²; population allocations use its share of 5,000,879 residents. Largest-remainder rounding with a one-block minimum keeps all 29 regions visible and each map’s total at 1,000. Rounded block shares differ slightly from exact shares; the table provides both.
  • Land map: the existing provincial silhouette is retained, with corrected regional block allocations. Division borders remain illustrative rather than survey-accurate. Coastal districts can have multiple parts; Mount Waddington and Strathcona include mainland territory as well as islands.
  • Population map: a schematic population cartogram. The north is compressed, the southwest mainland expands, and Vancouver Island is shown separately. Districts associated with Vancouver Island are grouped on the schematic island, including their mainland populations where applicable. Positions, boundaries and neighbouring relationships are approximate. This is not a density-equalizing projection.

How to read this kind of map

These are gridded cartograms, sometimes called block maps or tile grid maps. Instead of drawing each region at its real size, the whole province is split into equal blocks, and each region receives blocks according to its share of land area or population.

Equal blocks

Every block is the same size and stands for the same amount: 0.1% of B.C.’s land on the left map, and 0.1% of its people (about 5,000) on the right. A region’s size is simply how many blocks it has.

Why not a normal map?

A regular map shows land, so huge, thinly settled regions look important and small, crowded ones look minor. Equal blocks let every person count the same, so you can see where people actually live.

How to compare

Find a region on both maps. More blocks on the population map mean a larger share of people than of land. Metro Vancouver goes from 3 land blocks to 528 population blocks.

What it can’t show

A block is not a town or a place. Distances, shapes and regional borders are not to scale. The population layout preserves broad geographic relationships, but some adjacency changes and island-associated districts are grouped together. Use the maps for rounded shares and the table for exact figures.
